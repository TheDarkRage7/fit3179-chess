Chapter 01

A game without borders

From online play to international federations, explore where chess activity is recorded and how participation compares.

The growth of online chess

Lichess activity surged in 2020 and remained far above its earlier levels. Monthly rated games peaked at about 108.2 million in March 2023. By September 2026, activity was around 89.6 million games per month: below the peak, but still substantially higher than before 2020. The recent pattern shows fluctuations rather than continued rapid growth. These figures count games on Lichess, not unique players or all online chess. Hover to explore individual months.

Source: Lichess monthly standard rated game counts . Coverage: January 2013–September 2026.

Where are chess grandmasters?

Grandmasters are concentrated in European federations, with sizeable communities in countries such as the United States and India. Russia stands out with the largest circle, while many African federations have much smaller counts. Australia has 10 listed grandmasters, illustrating the smaller size of its elite chess community. Circle area represents the number of listed GM title holders; hover for federation totals. Affiliation does not necessarily indicate where a player lives.

Where is rated chess most widespread?

Adjusting for population reveals a different picture from simply counting players. Many European countries have high concentrations of listed rated players, while some populous countries have much lower rates. Australia has 4,644 listed players, equivalent to about 168 per million residents. This measures participation in the FIDE rating system, rather than everyone who plays chess casually or online. Chapter 02

Where does Australia fit?

Ratings provide another perspective on the chess community. Compare Australia's distribution with the world and selected federations. These charts use FIDE standard ratings.

How do Australian chess ratings compare?

Most listed players are concentrated towards the lower end of the published rating range, with progressively fewer players at higher ratings. Australia's largest 100-point band is 1600–1699, compared with 1500–1599 worldwide. In both groups, ratings above 2400 are uncommon. The distributions describe the players included in this list, rather than every chess player in either population. Switch between Australia and Worldwide to compare their shapes; hover within a column for counts and percentages.

Source: FIDE standard rating list . Downloaded 12 October 2026. Publication month not specified in the file. Includes active and inactive players with a published standard rating. Australia refers to Australian federation affiliation. The worldwide group includes Australia.

How do ratings vary across federations?

Australia's typical listed rating is similar to those of New Zealand and China, but below those of several other comparison federations. Australia's median is 1,678, meaning half its listed players have ratings at or below this value. Its middle 50% range from 1,561 to 1,823. Germany, the United States and England have higher medians in this list. These differences describe the composition of each federation's rated-player pool, rather than proving that one country's entire population is better at chess. Hover for exact values.

Source: FIDE standard rating list . Downloaded 12 October 2026. Publication month not specified in the file. Includes active and inactive standard-rated players, grouped by federation affiliation. Comparison federations are selected examples, not a complete ranking of countries.

Which rating groups are most common?

Australia and New Zealand have similar rating profiles, while the comparison federations show distinct concentrations. About 71.6% of Australia's listed players are rated below 1800. Germany's largest group is 1800–1999, whereas India's players are especially concentrated in the 1400–1599 band, at 67.5%. These percentages compare the mix within each federation, regardless of its total number of players. Darker cells indicate larger shares; hover for exact counts and percentages.

Chapter 03

The first move matters

What can Australian players learn from opening choices online? Explore a historical sample of Lichess games, from popular opening families to the first move and reply. This sample describes past games, not current platform-wide trends.

How do chess games begin?

The Sicilian Defense leads the individual opening families, but opening choices remain diverse. It accounts for 13.0% of this historical sample, followed by the French Defense at 7.0%. The 12 named families together represent about 55% of games; the remaining families collectively account for 45%. The large grey rectangle therefore represents many different families, not one dominant opening. Popularity describes what was played, not which opening offers the best chance of winning. Hover for full names and counts.

Source: Chess Game Dataset on Kaggle, collected from Lichess . Based on 15,117 unique rated games from the 2013–2017 dataset after excluding game IDs with conflicting details. Variations are grouped by the opening name before the colon. The top 12 families are shown separately; all others are combined. This sample does not represent current popularity across all Lichess games.

Do opening preferences change with rating?

The most popular opening families differ across rating groups in this sample. Among the six families shown, the King's Pawn Game ranks first below an average rating of 1200, but sixth in both groups averaging 1600 or more. The Sicilian Defense moves from fourth in the lowest group to first in every higher group. Ruy Lopez also rises from sixth to fourth. These are comparisons between groups, not evidence that individual players changed their choices as they improved. Click a legend entry to follow one opening.

Source: Chess Game Dataset on Kaggle, collected from Lichess . Uses the same cleaned historical sample of 15,117 rated games. Groups use the average of White and Black's Lichess ratings, which are separate from FIDE ratings. Rankings compare only the six families shown, not all openings. Differences between groups do not show players changing preferences over time.

White moves. How does Black reply?

White's e4 move dominates the start of games in this sample, but Black's responses branch into several paths. The largest individual connection is e4 followed by e5. Another substantial path connects e4 with c5, the starting moves of the Sicilian Defense. Games beginning with d4 most commonly connect to d5. The diagram shows how a small number of opening choices can lead into different game structures; it does not indicate which response is strongest. Hover over a path for its game count and percentage.

Reading the notation: e4 means a pawn moves to the e4 square; Nf6 means a knight moves to f6.

Source: Chess Game Dataset on Kaggle, collected from Lichess . Based on 15,111 unique rated games from the cleaned historical sample. Six games with fewer than two recorded moves were excluded. Less common first moves and replies are grouped as Other.

Chapter 04

On the world stage

International tournaments bring national teams together. Trace the Chess Olympiad's host cities, then explore how Australia performed against its starting seed in Budapest in 2024.

Where has the Chess Olympiad been hosted?

Every host city shown for 2000–2026 is in Europe or Asia. The map covers 13 completed editions across 12 cities, with Istanbul hosting twice. Chennai in 2022 and Samarkand in 2026 illustrate hosting beyond the European cluster. Australia did not host an edition during this period, although its teams participated internationally. Each dot represents one city, and its colour indicates the latest hosting period. Hover to see all hosting years.

Source: OlimpBase — Chess Olympiad history . The 2020 edition was cancelled. Markers use approximate city-centre coordinates, not exact tournament venues. Map boundaries: World Atlas / Natural Earth.

How did Australia perform at the 2024 Olympiad?

Australia's two teams finished on opposite sides of their starting seeds in Budapest in 2024. The women's team finished 36th after starting as the 46th seed, gaining ten places. The Open team finished 45th after starting 37th, finishing eight places below its seed. The results show that pre-tournament rankings do not determine final standings. Each team competed in a separate event, so their finishing positions should be interpreted within their own competition.

Read each line from left to right. Starting seed is the pre-tournament ranking based on player ratings; final position is the actual result. Lower numbers are better, so an upward line indicates a finish above the starting seed. The teams competed in separate events.

Source: Chess-Results, 2024 Open results and 2024 Women's results . Final standings after 11 rounds.
